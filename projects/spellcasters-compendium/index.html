A character sheet is a small record of an imagined life. What patterns appear when we read thousands of them together?

In Dungeons & Dragons, a class gives a character a set of abilities. This story focuses on six classes closely associated with magic—from a Wizard’s study to a Warlock’s pact.

The spellcasting classes

Wizards and Clerics are the largest groups in this six-class archive. Each accounts for less than one in five character IDs. The rest of the picture is spread across four other ways of making magic.

About the data

One person can make many characters. The source does not provide the player identities needed to count people.

The original 1.2 million rows include extensive repetition. The six-class population here uses one latest record per character ID.

Spells on the character sheet

A cantrip is a spell of level 0. These are the most commonly listed cantrips for each of the six classes in the smaller sheet collection. Vicious Mockery and Eldritch Blast stand out within the lists we can read.

03 /

A small decision
reverses the story.

The larger archive includes a note-length field. If we look only at records with positive lengths, Warlocks have the highest mean. Include valid recorded zeros, and Warlocks have the lowest.

That reversal says more about the measurement and its denominator than it can tell us about the people behind the sheets.

Interpreting zero values

Zero may mean no notes were recorded—or that the sheet was incomplete. The source does not resolve that ambiguity, so both policies stay visible.

All six medians are zero in the inclusive view. The precise source unit is unknown, and note length cannot measure storytelling effort or personality.

Reflection

This project began as an exploration of D&D character creation. Working with the data revealed important limits: repeated rows are not new characters, levels are not a timeline, and a missing field is not a motive.

The data reveals several patterns. Some labels recur, some spells are common in recorded lists, and a simple filtering choice can reverse a ranking. Making those choices visible gives the reader a way to judge the evidence.

These are selected historical online records, not a representative survey of D&D players. Campaign context, content access and incomplete sheets remain outside the data.
